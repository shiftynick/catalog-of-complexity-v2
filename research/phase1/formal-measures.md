# Formal Complexity Measures — Survey & Usability Verdicts

**Status:** Phase 1 research slice — formally defined complexity measures.
**Scope:** Judge each measure's usability as a metric-panel column for real, macroscopic, non-biological complex systems (the pilot 10: Sun, Hurricane Katrina, Earth's climate, San Andreas Fault, Mississippi basin, NYC, NYSE, the Internet, ERCOT, English).
**Organizing framework:** Seth Lloyd, "Measures of Complexity: A Nonexhaustive List," *IEEE Control Systems Magazine* 21(4), 2001, pp. 7–8.

Web verification was performed for essentially every claim below via search and page fetches; a small number of items could not be independently confirmed from primary sources (fetches failed or returned no usable text) and are explicitly marked **[unverified — model knowledge]**. No citation, DOI, page number, or numeric value has been invented — where I could not confirm a specific number I say so.

---

## 0. Lloyd's taxonomy, as actually found in the source

I was able to retrieve Lloyd's own list (MIT ESD.83 course notebook copy of the paper, `https://web.mit.edu/esd.83/www/notebook/Complexity.PDF`), which gives his three top-level groups verbatim:

**1. Difficulty of description** ("typically measured in bits") — Information, Entropy, Algorithmic Complexity / Algorithmic Information Content, Minimum Description Length, Fisher Information, Rényi Entropy, Code length (prefix-free, Huffman, Shannon-Fano, error-correcting, Hamming), Chernoff Information, Dimension, **Fractal Dimension**, Lempel-Ziv Complexity.

**2. Difficulty of creation** ("typically measured in time, energy, dollars, etc.") — Computational Complexity (time and space), Information-Based Complexity, **Logical Depth**, **Thermodynamic Depth**, Cost, Crypticity.

**3. Degree of organization**, split into two sub-lists:
- **(a) Effective complexity** (structure/organization side) — Metric Entropy, Fractal Dimension (appears again here), **Excess Entropy**, Stochastic Complexity, Sophistication, **Effective Measure Complexity**, "True" Measure Complexity, Topological ε-machine size (**statistical complexity**), Conditional Information, Conditional Algorithmic Information Content, Schema length, Ideal Complexity, Hierarchical Complexity, Tree subgraph diversity, Homogeneous Complexity, Grammatical Complexity.
- **(b) Mutual information** (parts-sharing-information side) — Algorithmic Mutual Information, Channel Capacity, Correlation, Stored Information, Organization.

Note that Lloyd uses "effective complexity" as a sub-heading label for the whole organization-of-structure family in this list — Gell-Mann & Lloyd's *specific* 1996 measure of the same name is one member of that family, not the heading itself; I treat them separately below to avoid conflating the two. **Multiscale entropy** (Costa/Goldberger/Peng, 2002) postdates Lloyd's list and is not in it; I have placed it in group 3(a) by construction since it is explicitly a multi-timescale extension of the "degree of organization / effective measure complexity" line of work.

I was not able to obtain a clean, complete text of the canonical journal-of-record version of Lloyd's paper (IEEE Control Systems Magazine 21(4):7–8, 2001) — the ScienceDirect/IEEE copy did not fetch as readable text — so this classification is sourced from the MIT course-notebook PDF copy plus corroborating secondary descriptions (Barbaresi's synthesis blog, ResearchGate abstract). The three-way top-level split (description / creation / organization) is corroborated by multiple independent secondary sources, giving reasonable confidence in that structure even though the exact copy I read is a course reproduction rather than the original magazine page scan.

## 1. Shannon entropy / entropy rate

**Lloyd group:** 1, Difficulty of description.

**Definition.** For a discrete random variable X with outcome probabilities p(x): H(X) = −Σ p(x) log₂ p(x), in bits. For a stochastic process (sequence of symbols), the **entropy rate** h = lim_{n→∞} H(X_n | X_1...X_{n-1}) is the asymptotic bits-per-symbol of uncertainty that remains even given unbounded history — the standard generalization to real, non-i.i.d. time series and sequences.

**Data required.** A symbol/state alphabet and either (a) the true or estimated probability distribution over symbols/states, or (b) a sufficiently long sequence from which n-gram or conditional-frequency statistics can be estimated (entropy-rate estimators are notoriously data-hungry — bias is severe unless the sequence length greatly exceeds the alphabet size to the power of the correlation length).

**Has it been computed for real macroscopic systems? Yes, extensively — of all nine measures this is the most computed on real data.**
- **English text:** Shannon's own 1951 experiment estimated H ≈ 1.3 bits/character (range roughly 0.6–1.3 bpc depending on method) via a guessing-game with human subjects on passages from *Jefferson the Virginian* (C. E. Shannon, "Prediction and Entropy of Printed English," *Bell System Technical Journal* 30(1):50–64, 1951). More recent replications and refinements: Moradi/Grzymala-Busse-style and Mahoney's "Refining the Estimated Entropy of English by Shannon Game Simulation" (mattmahoney.net); Bentz et al., "Entropy Rate Estimation for English via a Large Cognitive Experiment Using Mechanical Turk," *Entropy* 21(12):1201 (2019) — the crowdsourced Mechanical Turk replication of Shannon's experiment at scale, giving a modern, larger-sample entropy-rate estimate for English.
- **Climate/weather time series:** multiple recent applications compute Shannon entropy on temperature and precipitation records, e.g. "The Dynamics of Shannon Entropy in Analyzing Climate Variability for Modeling Temperature and Precipitation Uncertainty in Poland," *Entropy* 27(4):398 (2025), and "Application of Shannon Entropy in Assessing Changes in Precipitation Conditions and Temperature Based on Long-Term Sequences Using the Bootstrap Method," *Atmosphere* 15(8):898 (2024).
- **Financial markets:** entropy of price/volatility time series computed on tick-by-tick data 1999–2004 across six markets (found via search; specific journal/authors not independently confirmed — **[partially unverified: general claim corroborated by multiple entropy-of-markets papers, but I could not pin the exact 1999–2004 six-market study to a specific citation]**). Better-verified: "Measuring market efficiency: The Shannon entropy of high-frequency financial time series," *Chaos, Solitons & Fractals* (2022), ScienceDirect.

**Verdict: USABLE-AS-COLUMN.** Shannon entropy / entropy rate is the best-instrumented measure on this whole list for exactly the kind of macroscopic systems in this catalog: it has been computed on language corpora, climate records, and financial tick data, using nothing more exotic than a time series and a symbolization/binning scheme. The caveat for the panel: entropy rate is **not invariant to the choice of alphabet, binning, or sampling resolution**, so cross-system comparison requires the schema to pin down a specific estimator and discretization convention (this is a "commensurability rule" problem, not a computability problem).

## 2. Kolmogorov (algorithmic) complexity

**Lloyd group:** 1, Difficulty of description (as "Algorithmic Complexity / Algorithmic Information Content").

**Definition.** K(x) = the length of the shortest program for a fixed universal Turing machine U that outputs string x and halts. Formally uncomputable (no algorithm can compute K(x) for arbitrary x — a consequence of the halting problem); only upper bounds are obtainable (any program that produces x, e.g. a compressed file, is an upper bound on K(x)).

**Data required.** In principle: none beyond the string itself and a reference universal machine — but since it is uncomputable, in practice: the string plus a real-world lossless compressor used as a proxy.

**Has it been computed for real macroscopic systems?** Never computed exactly (as is mathematically guaranteed never to happen for any nontrivial object). **Approximated** routinely via compression-based proxies:
- **Normalized Compression Distance (NCD):** Cilibrasi & Vitányi, "Clustering by Compression," *IEEE Transactions on Information Theory* 51(4):1523–1545 (2005) — defines NCD(x,y) = [C(xy) − min(C(x),C(y))] / max(C(x),C(y)) using a real compressor C, as a computable stand-in for the (uncomputable) normalized information distance built from K(x).
- **Real applications found:** NCD used for whole-genome phylogeny reconstruction of SARS-CoV-2 (Cilibrasi et al., "Fast Whole-Genome Phylogeny of the COVID-19 Virus SARS-CoV-2 by Compression," *bioRxiv* 2020.07.22.216242, later PMC9030035) and protein sequence classification (Bioinformatics, 2005, "Application of compression-based distance measures to protein sequence classification"). These are biological/genomic examples (out of this project's scope per the brief) but they are the best-verified "real data, not toy strings" applications of a Kolmogorov-complexity proxy that I could confirm. I did not find a comparably well-cited application of NCD/compression-based K-estimation to a macroscopic *non-biological* system (climate record, market tape, city street network) — searches on "Kolmogorov complexity … climate/genome" returned mostly genomic hits and one general claim that "systems characterized by meaningful complex patterns … possess high effective complexity" without a specific citation I could verify.

**Verdict: MENTION-ONLY.** Exact K(x) is provably uncomputable — this is not a data-availability problem but a mathematical one, so no citation will ever give a "true" Kolmogorov complexity value for any of our systems. Compression-based proxies (NCD, LZMA/BZIP2 description length) are real and published, but the best-attested large-scale applications are biological (genomes, viral phylogeny), which are out of this project's scope; I could not verify a compression-based K-estimate computed on any of the ten pilot systems' native data (e.g., compressed length of ERCOT's grid topology, or of a Mississippi hydrograph) as an existing published number. Worth a panel *footnote or narrative mention* (it is the conceptual root that Lempel-Ziv complexity, MDL, and NCD all approximate) but not usable as a fillable quantitative column.

## 3. Logical depth (Bennett)

**Lloyd group:** 2, Difficulty of creation.

**Definition.** Bennett's logical depth of a string x at significance level s is the minimum runtime T(p) over all programs p that produce x on a universal machine U and whose length is within s bits of the shortest program p* for x: depth_s(x) = min{ T(p) : |p| − |p*| < s, U(p) = x }. Intuitively: how long it takes a near-optimally-compact description to unfold into the object — deep objects have a short algorithmic description but require a long causal/computational history to actually generate (C. H. Bennett, "Logical Depth and Physical Complexity," in *The Universal Turing Machine: A Half-Century Survey*, 1988).

**Data required.** In the strict formal sense: the shortest program for x (itself uncomputable, per Kolmogorov complexity above) plus its runtime — so logical depth inherits uncomputability from K(x) and adds an extra layer (you also need to know you have found a *near*-shortest program, which requires solving/approximating the same undecidable problem).

**Has it been computed for real macroscopic systems?** I found **no confirmed instance** of logical depth being numerically computed for any real macroscopic physical, geophysical, or socio-economic system. The literature I could retrieve (Wikipedia "Logical depth," Bennett's original chapter, and secondary discussion in "Measuring complexity" arXiv:1909.13243, and "Effective Complexity and its Relation to Logical Depth" arXiv:0810.5663) uses logical depth **qualitatively** — e.g., illustrative discussion of a protein's structure (comparing a "print coordinates verbatim" program vs. an "ab initio quantum chemistry" program) is used to *argue* that biological structures are logically deep, not to *compute* a depth value. This protein example is itself a thought experiment, not a measured number, and biological besides. No published depth value in bits/steps for a star, a hurricane, a fault system, or a market that I could locate.

**Verdict: MENTION-ONLY.** Logical depth is philosophically important — it is the cleanest formalization of "complex things take a long causal history to make," which resonates strongly with this project's whole framing (Chaisson's cosmic evolution, historical/path-dependence column). But it is doubly uncomputable in the strict sense and I could not verify a single published numeric estimate for any real large-scale system, biological or not. Recommend: keep as a **narrative/conceptual reference** in the metrics doc (it motivates a rubric column like "history/path dependence"), not as a fillable column.

## 4. Thermodynamic depth (Lloyd & Pagels)

**Lloyd group:** 2, Difficulty of creation.

**Definition.** For a system with a well-defined set of macroscopic states and a stochastic (Markovian) dynamics connecting them, thermodynamic depth of a state is proportional to the (Shannon) entropy of the ensemble of possible histories/trajectories that could have led to that state — equivalently, for a Hamiltonian system, the difference between the coarse-grained and fine-grained entropy. Ordered states (one dominant history) and wholly random states (entropy already maximal, no informative history) are both shallow; states reachable by only a few distinguishable, resolved historical paths are deep. Source: S. Lloyd & H. Pagels, "Complexity as Thermodynamic Depth," *Annals of Physics* 188(1):186–213 (1988).

**Data required.** A specified partition of the system into macroscopic states, transition probabilities/dynamics between them, and enough history to estimate the entropy of the trajectory ensemble leading to the observed state — i.e., a full stochastic-process model of the system's history, not just a snapshot.

**Has it been computed for real macroscopic systems?** I could not find a verified numeric thermodynamic-depth value computed for any real physical system (star, hurricane, market, etc.). The clearest documented criticism, which doubles as an explanation for the absence of real applications, is Shalizi's: thermodynamic depth "is fundamentally flawed because it relies on a set of chosen macroscopic states for the system, which are difficult to choose, and Lloyd and Pagels does not mention how these states are supposed to be chosen" (C. R. Shalizi, discussed in "Thermodynamic Depth of Causal States: When Paddling around in Occam's Pool Shallowness Is a Virtue," arXiv cond-mat/9808147, Shalizi & Crutchfield). That paper explicitly proposes replacing the ad hoc macrostates in thermodynamic depth with causal states (i.e., ε-machine states) precisely because the original formulation left state-choice undefined and thus unusable in practice.

**Verdict: MENTION-ONLY, borderline UNUSABLE.** Even Lloyd's own co-author lineage (Crutchfield, via Shalizi) treats thermodynamic depth as a good idea that was never operationalized because it depends on an unspecified, subjective macrostate partition. No real-system numeric example found. Include in the report as a historically important stepping-stone toward statistical complexity (which fixes this exact problem via ε-machines) but not as a panel column.

## 5. Effective complexity (Gell-Mann & Lloyd)

**Lloyd group:** 3(a), Degree of organization.

**Definition.** Effective complexity (EC) of an entity is the algorithmic information content (Kolmogorov complexity) of the *regularities* of the entity — formally, one describes the entity as a member of an ensemble defined by its regular/non-random features, and EC is roughly K(description of the ensemble's regularities), separating that from the residual algorithmic randomness (which contributes to total algorithmic information content but not to EC). A purely ordered string and a purely random string both have low EC; a string with structure at many scales has high EC. Source: M. Gell-Mann & S. Lloyd, "Information Measures, Effective Complexity, and Total Information," *Complexity* 2(1):44–52 (1996).

**Data required.** A judgment call on which features of the system count as "regularity" vs. "noise," followed by (in principle) a Kolmogorov-complexity computation on the regularity-description — so it inherits the uncomputability of K(x) *and* adds a prior, subjective step of separating signal from noise.

**Has it been computed for real macroscopic systems?** No numeric EC value for a real system was found. Confirmed from Wikipedia's "Effective complexity" page: the measure "has been criticised as being dependent on the subjective decisions made as to which parts of the information in the system are to be discounted as random" — i.e., the definition itself is widely acknowledged as not admitting a unique, reproducible number without first fixing (subjectively) what counts as regularity. Related theoretical literature (arXiv:0810.5663 "Effective Complexity and its Relation to Logical Depth"; arXiv:1001.2686 "Effective complexity of stationary process realizations") works with the concept formally/asymptotically but does not report a computed value for an empirical macroscopic system.

**Verdict: MENTION-ONLY.** EC is conceptually central to this project (it is literally trying to formalize "the amount of non-random structure," which is close to the spirit of the whole Catalog), but it inherits Kolmogorov uncomputability and adds a subjective regularity/randomness split on top. No real-system computation found anywhere. Good candidate for narrative discussion in the metrics doc's rationale section, not a fillable column.

## 6. Statistical complexity / ε-machines (Crutchfield & Young)

**Lloyd group:** 3(a), Degree of organization ("Topological ε-machine size").

**Definition.** Computational mechanics (Crutchfield & Young, "Inferring Statistical Complexity," *Physical Review Letters* 63(2):105–108, 1989) reconstructs the minimal, maximally-predictive hidden-Markov model of a process — the **ε-machine** — whose states ("causal states") are equivalence classes of pasts that yield the same conditional distribution over futures. Statistical complexity Cμ = H[S], the Shannon entropy of the stationary distribution over causal states S, i.e., the number of bits needed to specify which causal state the process is in. Provably the ε-machine is the unique minimal optimal predictor (three optimality theorems: sufficiency, minimality, uniqueness), which distinguishes Cμ from ad hoc Markov-model complexity.

**Data required.** A discretized/symbolized time series (or spatial field) long enough to reliably estimate the conditional-future distributions needed to infer causal-state equivalence classes — reconstruction algorithms (e.g., CSSR) need substantially more data than a simple entropy-rate estimate because they are effectively doing structural (not just distributional) inference.

**Has it been computed for real macroscopic systems? Yes — this is the strongest "MENTION-worthy but borderline usable" case among the abstract measures.**
- **Geomagnetic data:** R. W. Clarke, M. P. Freeman, N. W. Watkins, "The Application of Computational Mechanics to the Analysis of Geomagnetic Data," published version in *Physical Review E* (arXiv cond-mat/0110228) — ε-machine reconstruction applied to a real geomagnetic time series recorded at Halley, Antarctica (British Antarctic Survey), recovering structure interpreted as the diurnal solar-facing current pattern plus stochastic magnetic-substorm signatures.
- **Atmospheric turbulence:** statistical complexity and mutual information computed on two real turbulence datasets — ocean-surface-wind time series and a time-height Doppler-lidar image of vertical winds — with elevated complexity found in regimes where standard turbulence statistical models perform poorly, and at the unstable-to-stable boundary-layer transition. **[The specific author/journal pairing for this turbulence study could not be independently pinned down in my searches — the finding is corroborated by multiple secondary summaries but I did not reach the primary paper's bibliographic details, so treat the *existence* of this application as reasonably confirmed but the exact citation as unverified.]**
- Broader corroborated (secondary-source) claim: computational mechanics/ε-machines have also been applied to crystallographic data, molecular dynamics, and single-molecule microscopy — plausible and consistent with the computational-mechanics literature, but I could not independently verify specific citations for each within this research pass.

**Verdict: USABLE-AS-COLUMN, with a significant asterisk / MENTION-ONLY in practice for most of our ten systems.** Unlike thermodynamic depth or effective complexity, statistical complexity *has* been computed on real geophysical time series (geomagnetism, turbulence) by named, citable groups. In principle it could be computed for several pilot systems that already have long, well-sampled time series: Earth's climate (temperature/circulation indices), the NYSE (price/return sequences — computational-mechanics-style symbolic dynamics has precedent in finance-adjacent complexity literature), possibly Katrina-class systems via reanalysis wind fields. However, no one has actually published a Cμ value for any of our ten named pilot systems specifically, and ε-machine reconstruction is technically demanding (alphabet/discretization choices, data-length requirements, no off-the-shelf standardized tool comparable to a compressor). Recommend flagging as **MENTION-ONLY for the v1 panel**, but revisit as USABLE for a future phase if the team is willing to commission a fresh reconstruction on, e.g., ERCOT load time series or an ENSO/climate index — the closest thing to a "known compute recipe" among the deep information-theoretic measures.

## 7. Excess entropy / predictive information / effective measure complexity (Grassberger)

**Lloyd group:** 3(a), Degree of organization.

**Definition.** Excess entropy E is the mutual information between a process's entire semi-infinite past and its entire semi-infinite future: E = I(Past; Future). Equivalently, it is the sub-extensive part of the block entropy H(L) as block length L → ∞: H(L) ≈ E + h·L, where h is the entropy rate — E is the finite offset/intercept term left over after subtracting the extensive (linear-in-L) part. Grassberger's original "effective measure complexity" (P. Grassberger, "Toward a Quantitative Theory of Self-Generated Complexity," *International Journal of Theoretical Physics* 25:907–938, 1986) and Bialek, Nemenman & Tishby's "predictive information" (in "Predictability, Complexity, and Learning," *Neural Computation* 13:2409–2463, 2001 — Princeton preprint version confirmed) are essentially the same quantity under different names, and it is provably a lower bound on statistical complexity Cμ (E ≤ Cμ, with equality for "unifilar" processes reconstructable without extra hidden structure).

**Data required.** A long symbol sequence (or discretized time series) from which block entropies H(L) can be estimated for a range of L, then extrapolated — this is data-hungry in the same way entropy-rate estimation is, and more so because you need the *sub-extensive* correction term, which requires estimating and subtracting a much larger extensive term.

**Has it been computed for real macroscopic systems?** Grassberger's own 1986 paper gives worked numerical estimates for cellular automata and iterated maps (toy systems, not macroscopic real-world data). For real time series, I found qualitative/methodological discussion (e.g., "using the excess entropy as a complexity measure for continuous valued time series has to deal with the fact that its value will be different for different partitions" — a direct, confirmed statement of the partition-dependence problem) but **no specific numeric excess-entropy value computed for a named real macroscopic system** (no verified E-in-bits for a climate record, a market tape, or a seismic catalog) in the sources I could retrieve. This is consistent with excess entropy's role in the literature as more of a *theoretical bound / diagnostic concept* (used to prove Cμ ≥ E, or to argue about the "complexity" of stochastic processes analytically) than an empirically reported number for real instrumented systems.

**Verdict: MENTION-ONLY.** Well-defined, theoretically important (it's the rigorous version of "how much does the past tell you about the future," directly relevant to this project's "memory / information storage" panel column), and computable in principle from any sufficiently long real time series — but I could not verify that anyone has actually published an excess-entropy number for one of our pilot systems or a comparable macroscopic system. The partition/discretization dependence is a confirmed, acknowledged practical obstacle. Recommend narrative mention only, cross-referenced against the "memory/history" rubric column rather than treated as its own quantitative column.

## 8. Multiscale entropy (Costa, Goldberger & Peng)

**Lloyd group:** not in Lloyd's original 2001 list (postdates it); placed here under 3(a), degree of organization, as a multi-timescale generalization of the entropy-rate/effective-measure-complexity family.

**Definition.** Multiscale entropy (MSE) computes sample entropy (a finite-data entropy-rate proxy) not on the raw time series but on a family of "coarse-grained" versions of it, produced by averaging the series in non-overlapping windows of increasing scale factor τ = 1, 2, 3, .... The result is a *curve* of entropy vs. scale, rather than a single number — systems with meaningful structure across multiple timescales show entropy that stays high or rises across scales, while both fully random (white) noise and fully ordered signals show entropy that falls off quickly with τ. Source: M. Costa, A. L. Goldberger, C.-K. Peng, "Multiscale Entropy Analysis of Complex Physiologic Time Series," *Physical Review Letters* 89(6):068102 (2002).

**Data required.** A single long, evenly-sampled time series (the coarse-graining/windowing procedure is simple to apply to any scalar time series) — comparatively modest data demands versus ε-machine reconstruction or excess entropy, since sample entropy at each scale is a well-established, tractable finite-sample estimator.

**Has it been computed for real macroscopic systems?** Extensively, but the confirmed applications are overwhelmingly **physiological/biological** — the originating and dominant application domain is heart-rate variability (ECG/RR-interval series distinguishing healthy vs. pathologic cardiac dynamics), plus arterial blood pressure and lung volume series (per PubMed 17950584), fetal heart rate during labor as an acidemia biomarker, and diabetes-related glucose dynamics. These are explicitly out of scope per this project's brief (biological systems). For non-biological macroscopic systems, I found one directly relevant hit: "Multi-Scale Entropy Analysis as a Method for Time-Series Analysis of Climate Data" (ResearchGate) — confirming MSE has been applied to climate time series, though I could not extract the specific numeric multiscale-entropy values or full citation details (journal/year) from the search snippet alone.

**Verdict: MENTION-ONLY, leaning UNUSABLE for the current panel.** The method itself is computationally simple and could technically be applied to any of our systems' long time series (Katrina's minute-by-minute pressure trace, ERCOT load curves, NYSE tick data, Mississippi discharge records), and one non-biological climate application was found. But (a) essentially all of the well-established, heavily-cited MSE literature is physiological and therefore off-topic per this project's exclusion of biological systems, (b) MSE produces a *curve*, not a scalar, which is awkward for a single-value panel column (would need to be reduced to a summary statistic, e.g. complexity index = area under the curve, an extra non-standardized step), and (c) I could not verify a specific published MSE value for any pilot system. Recommend mention-only, with a note that it is a promising method the project *could* commission fresh computation on (e.g., ERCOT or NYSE data) if a "multi-timescale predictability" column is wanted later.

## 9. Fractal dimension

**Lloyd group:** 1, Difficulty of description (also reappears in 3(a), degree of organization).

**Definition.** Multiple non-identical operationalizations share the name: (a) **Hausdorff/box-counting dimension** D, typically estimated empirically via the Richardson/box-counting relation N(ε) ∝ ε^(−D), where N(ε) is the number of boxes of size ε needed to cover the object, and D is the slope of log N(ε) vs. log(1/ε); (b) the **Hurst exponent H** for self-affine time series, related to a local fractal dimension via D = 2 − H for a 1-D signal graph. These measure how a geometric or statistical property (length, count, roughness) scales non-integrally with the measurement resolution — this is the historically original "measure of complexity" for real physical objects, predating most of the information-theoretic measures above.

**Data required.** A geometric object or map (coastline, fault trace, river network, urban footprint) measured at multiple resolutions/scales, or a scalar time series long enough to compute rescaled-range (R/S) or wavelet-based Hurst-exponent estimates.

**Has it been computed for real macroscopic systems? Yes — by far the most real-world-computed measure on this entire list, with direct hits for at least four of our ten pilot systems.**
- **Coastlines (foundational case, general precedent for geophysical boundaries):** L. F. Richardson's empirical length-vs-scale observations, formalized by B. Mandelbrot, "How Long Is the Coast of Britain? Statistical Self-Similarity and Fractional Dimension," *Science* 156(3775):636–638 (1967) — reports D ≈ 1.25 for the west coast of Great Britain. (Confirmed via multiple secondary sources; I was not able to fetch the original *Science* article text directly, but the D=1.25 figure for the British west coast is consistently and independently reported across sources describing Mandelbrot's paper.)
- **San Andreas Fault (direct pilot-system hit):** K. Aviles, C. H. Scholz, J. Boatwright, "Fractal Analysis Applied to Characteristic Segments of the San Andreas Fault," *Journal of Geophysical Research: Solid Earth* 92(B1):331–344 (1987), and P. G. Okubo & K. Aki, "Fractal Geometry in the San Andreas Fault System," *JGR: Solid Earth* 92(B1):345–355 (1987) — report scale-dependent D values along the fault: D ≈ 1.1 near Parkfield, D ≈ 1.3 for a 30-km-wide band around the main trace at 1:750,000 map scale, and D ≈ 1.4 for the San Andreas and related faults near San Bernardino; a separate logistic-fit study found D ≈ 1.29.
- **River networks (direct pilot-system hit, general precedent for Mississippi basin):** Horton's laws of stream network composition are explicitly connected to fractal dimension via D = log(R_B)/log(R_L) (bifurcation ratio over length ratio), typically D ≈ 1.9 for real drainage networks (multiple hydrology sources, e.g. Beer & Borgas-type "Horton's Laws and the Fractal Nature of Streams," *Water Resources Research* 29(3), 1993). A specific regional measurement found: fractal dimensions of river lengths in Missouri (part of the Mississippi basin) ranging 1.036–1.291, average 1.158 — found via search of academic river-network fractal literature; **[the exact primary citation for the Missouri 1.036–1.291 figure could not be independently pinned down beyond the search-engine summary, so treat that specific number as corroborated-but-not-directly-verified pending a follow-up primary-source check]**.
- **Cities / urban form (direct pilot-system hit):** box-counting dimension of urban street networks and built-up area is a well-established GIS technique (e.g., "Box-Counting Dimension of Fractal Urban Form: Stability Issues and Measurement Design," *International Journal of Artificial Life Research* 3(3), 2012; "Realizing the Box-Counting Method for Calculating Fractal Dimension of Urban Form Based on Remote Sensing Image," *Geo-spatial Information Science* 12(1), 2009). I could not confirm a specific published D value for New York City's street network itself in this pass (case studies I could confirm by name were Beijing, Singapore, and various European cities), but the method is directly and repeatedly applied to city street networks generally, so a value for NYC is very likely obtainable from the urban-morphology literature even though I did not verify a specific NYC number today.
- **Financial markets / NYSE (direct pilot-system hit):** Hurst-exponent/fractal-dimension analysis of stock indices is a large, active literature. Confirmed figures: D = 1.5 corresponds to an uncorrelated (efficient-market) process; empirical studies of stock indices find most below D = 1.5 (indicating persistence/momentum), with the least efficient markets falling below D = 1.4; specific computed values reported include D = 1.1894 (cell-covering method) and D = 1.6 (Hurst-exponent-derived) in at least one comparative study of stock indices (ScienceDirect, "Analysis on Historical Data of Stock Indices Using Fractal Dimension Methods by Measuring the Complexity," 2025). A dedicated S&P 500 study exists: "Estimating the Fractal Dimension of the S&P 500 Index Using Wavelet Analysis" (Princeton working paper / arXiv math-0703834) — confirms the S&P 500 specifically (not just "a" stock index) has been fractal-dimension-analyzed, though I did not extract its headline D value from the search snippet.

**Verdict: USABLE-AS-COLUMN.** By a wide margin, fractal dimension is the best-attested formal complexity measure for this project's purposes: it has multiple independent, decades-deep literatures computing real numeric D values for coastlines/geophysical boundaries (the general case), the San Andreas Fault specifically, river networks generally and the Mississippi basin's Missouri sub-basin specifically, urban street networks generally (methodology directly transferable to NYC even without a verified NYC-specific number in hand today), and stock market indices generally and the S&P 500 specifically (a reasonable proxy/adjacent series for NYSE-listed-market dynamics). The main caveat for the panel schema: "fractal dimension" is not one measurement protocol but a family (box-counting vs. Hurst-derived vs. mainstream-length-vs-area scaling), and values are scale-range-dependent (the coastline paradox itself), so the schema needs to record *which* estimator and *what scale range* was used alongside the number — a commensurability concern, not a computability one.

## Biggest uncertainties

1. **I could not obtain clean readable text of the canonical journal version of Lloyd's own 2001 paper** (IEEE Control Systems Magazine 21(4):7–8) — the taxonomy above is drawn from an MIT course-notebook PDF reproduction plus corroborating secondary sources, not the original magazine scan. The three-top-level-group structure is well corroborated across independent sources, but I cannot fully rule out small differences (omissions, reorderings) between the notebook copy and the original two-page magazine article.

2. **Several "no real-world computation found" verdicts (logical depth, thermodynamic depth, effective complexity, excess entropy) are negative claims based on a bounded search pass, not an exhaustive literature review.** It is plausible that a numeric application exists somewhere in specialized physics/complexity-theory venues that my searches did not surface — these verdicts should be read as "not found despite a genuine attempt," not "proven not to exist." A dedicated follow-up search (e.g., against Web of Science / Google Scholar citation-forward search from the original papers) could overturn some of these.

3. **A handful of specific numeric claims are only search-snippet-corroborated, not primary-source-verified**, and are flagged inline: the Missouri river-network fractal dimension range (1.036–1.291), the 1999–2004 six-market entropy study, the atmospheric-turbulence statistical-complexity study's exact authorship, and the climate-data multiscale-entropy paper's full citation. These should be re-verified against primary sources before being entered into the panel as citable quantitative values — right now they are good enough to inform the USABLE/MENTION verdicts but not yet good enough to cite in a published entry.
