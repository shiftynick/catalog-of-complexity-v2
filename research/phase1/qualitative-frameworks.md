# Qualitative "Features of Complex Systems" Frameworks — Consolidated Research

**Prepared for:** Catalog of Complexity (COC) project, Phase 1 — rubric-scored column design
**Scope:** Extract and consolidate feature/property lists from major qualitative complexity frameworks, to serve as the basis for 0–4 ordinal rubric columns applied uniformly across all catalog systems (Sun, Hurricane Katrina, Earth's climate, San Andreas Fault, Mississippi basin, NYC, NYSE, the Internet, ERCOT, English).

Nothing below is fabricated; anything not corroborated by an actual fetched source is explicitly flagged **[unverified — model knowledge]**.

## Part (a): Per-Framework Feature Lists

### 1. Ladyman, Lambert & Wiesner, "What is a Complex System?" (*European Journal for Philosophy of Science*, 2013, Vol. 3, pp. 33–67, DOI: 10.1007/s13194-012-0056-8)

Also later expanded into the book *What Is a Complex System?* by Ladyman & Wiesner (Yale University Press, 2020), which appears to retain substantially the same feature list in its chapter "Features of Complex Systems."

**Source confidence:** High for the list itself — independently corroborated across three sources: a WebSearch-indexed excerpt of the original paper, a detailed philosophy-blog reading-notes post (joshuaj.net, "Notes on Ladyman, Lambert and Wiesner"), and a formal book review (David Kinney, *BJPS Review of Books*, "Ladyman and Wiesner, *What Is a Complex System?*", thebsps.org). Direct PDF fetch of the original paper (philsci-archive.pitt.edu/9044/4/LLWultimate.pdf) failed due to PDF encoding issues, so exact page numbers and full verbatim quotes could not be independently confirmed — treat specific quoted phrases below as reported by secondary sources, not confirmed against the primary text directly by this research pass.

LLW's method: they survey the complexity literature, identify a recurring cluster of features associated with "complex system" as used across physics, biology, and social science, and ask of each whether it is **necessary**, **sufficient**, both, or neither for a system to count as complex. They explicitly do **not** end up with a full set of necessary-and-sufficient conditions; instead they argue complex systems form a *cluster kind* (later, in the 2020 book, argued to be a *natural kind*) picked out by a weighted combination of these features.

**The ten features (as consolidated across sources):**

1. **Numerosity** — the system has many interacting components/parts. "More than a few, and often many elements are required." Numerosity is tied to the "more is different" idea: some phenomena only appear once the number of interacting parts crosses some scale. LLW note there is no precise quantitative threshold given.
2. **Disorder and diversity** — the components are heterogeneous and there is no centralized controller dictating their behavior.
3. **Feedback** — the behavior of interacting components depends on interaction with neighboring components at an earlier time (iterated, often nonlinear, causal loops).
4. **Non-equilibrium behaviour** — the system is thermodynamically/dynamically open to its environment and operates away from equilibrium.
5. **Spontaneous order and self-organization** — ordered macroscopic patterns emerge from the disordered interactions of components without external/centralized direction.
6. **Non-linearity** — components' collective outputs are not simply additive functions of individual inputs (small causes can have disproportionate effects, or vice versa).
7. **Robustness (and lack of central control)** — the emergent organization is resistant/robust to perturbation of components; redundancy allows the system to persist despite damage to parts, without a central controller managing this resilience.
8. **Nested structure and modularity** — hierarchical organization, with semi-autonomous subsystems/modules nested within larger structures.
9. **History and memory** — the system's current state/behavior depends on its past trajectory (path dependence); "memory" distributed in the system's structure rather than a single location.
10. **Adaptive behaviour** — the capacity of the system (or its components) to adjust behavior in response to the environment, e.g., via learning or evolution.

**Necessary vs. sufficient — reported treatment (moderate-to-high confidence, cross-corroborated by two independent sources):**
- **Necessary** (per the BJPS review by Kinney): numerosity, spontaneous order/self-organization, feedback, and robustness are each treated as necessary conditions for complexity.
- **Neither necessary nor sufficient** (explicitly, per the same review and the joshuaj.net notes): disorder/diversity, non-linearity, and adaptive behaviour. The joshuaj.net notes specifically state LLW call non-linearity "neither necessary nor sufficient" for complexity, though it plays a supporting role.
- **Ambiguous / not clearly resolved** in secondary summaries: non-equilibrium behaviour, nested structure/modularity, and history/memory.
- One secondary source paraphrases a stronger textual claim: "Emergence in all epistemological senses is necessary, but it is not sufficient, for complex systems" — note that **emergence** itself is treated by LLW as a separate, closely related but distinct concept from the ten-item list above (they discuss emergence largely in connection with feature #5 and #7, and devote separate treatment to defining/critiquing "emergence" as vague when used loosely). Confidence: single-source paraphrase, not independently corroborated verbatim — treat cautiously.
- A working definition attributed to LLW by one source: "A complex system is an ensemble of many elements which are interacting in a disordered way, resulting in robust organization and memory." This should be treated as a paraphrase pending primary-source confirmation, not a confirmed direct quote.
- The 2020 book review states the authors ultimately argue complex systems form a *natural kind*, characterized by clusters of these emergent features rather than a strict necessary-and-sufficient definition.

**Citations:**
- Ladyman, J., Lambert, J., & Wiesner, K. (2013). "What is a complex system?" *European Journal for Philosophy of Science*, 3(1), 33–67. https://doi.org/10.1007/s13194-012-0056-8
- Ladyman, J., & Wiesner, K. (2020). *What Is a Complex System?* New Haven, CT: Yale University Press. ISBN 978-0-300-25110-4.
- Secondary sources consulted: Kinney, D., review of Ladyman & Wiesner, *BJPS Review of Books* (thebsps.org/reviewofbooks/kinney-on-ladyman-and-wiesner/); Johnson, J., "'What is a Complex System?': Notes on Ladyman, Lambert, and Wiesner (Part 1)" (joshuaj.net/text/what-is-a-complex-system/).

### 2. Melanie Mitchell, *Complexity: A Guided Tour* (Oxford University Press, 2009)

**Source confidence:** High. Corroborated across three independent secondary sources (jenal.org review, blas.com "Rabbit Hole" review, jasss.soc.surrey.ac.uk formal book review), which converge closely on wording.

Mitchell's working definition (reported as a close/exact quote by the blas.com review, cross-checked against the jasss.soc.surrey.ac.uk review's paraphrase, which independently agrees on substance):

> "A complex system is a system in which large networks of components with no central control and simple rules of operation give rise to complex collective behavior, sophisticated information processing, and adaptation via learning or evolution."

She frames this explicitly as a **working/provisional** definition, not a final formal one — Mitchell is on record (via the jasss and jenal reviews) as acknowledging that complexity science lacks a single agreed-upon definition or measure of complexity, closing the book by noting the field is "still not a unified whole but rather a collection of disparate parts with some overlapping concepts."

**The three core generic properties** she identifies as common across complex systems (ant colonies, brains, immune systems, economies, the WWW) — corroborated by two independent sources:
1. **Complex collective behavior** — organized, often surprising macro-level patterns/behavior arising from interaction among many simple components, without central direction.
2. **Signaling and information processing** — components/system process and exchange information, and the system as a whole can be seen as processing information about its environment.
3. **Adaptation** — the system (or its components) changes behavior to improve its chances of success/survival, via learning, evolution, or other adaptive processes.

**Additional recurring concepts she discusses at length in the book** (per the jenal.org review's fuller list; treat as descriptive vocabulary she uses/explains rather than a second formal enumerated "properties list"): emergence, self-organization, feedback, nonlinearity, sensitivity to initial conditions, criticality/"edge of chaos" dynamics, symmetry breaking, resilience, evolution. One source characterizes a "definition of complex systems" in the book as: systems that "exhibit nontrivial emergent and self-organizing behaviors, in which order is created out of disorder" — this is a paraphrase from secondary material, flagged as such.

**Citations:**
- Mitchell, M. (2009). *Complexity: A Guided Tour*. Oxford: Oxford University Press. ISBN 978-0199798100.
- Secondary sources consulted: Jenal, M., "Melanie Mitchell: Complexity – A Guided Tour" (jenal.org/melanie-mitchell-complexity-a-guided-tour/); "Complexity: A Guided Tour by Melanie Mitchell" (blas.com/complexity-mitchell/); review, *Journal of Artificial Societies and Social Simulation* 13(2) (jasss.soc.surrey.ac.uk/13/2/reviews/3.html).

### 3. John Holland, *Hidden Order: How Adaptation Builds Complexity* (Addison-Wesley/Helix Books, 1995)

**Source confidence:** High. Corroborated across two independent, detailed secondary sources (a book review at serendipstudio.org and an independent WebSearch-indexed summary), which agree closely on structure and definitions.

Holland's framework, presented as "The Seven Basics" (roughly the first third of the book), organizes into **four properties** and **three mechanisms** shared by all complex adaptive systems (CAS):

**Properties:**
1. **Aggregation** — used in two related senses: (a) a cognitive/modeling sense — the ability to categorize/group similar things and treat them as equivalent classes rather than tracking every individual specific ("they are all cars"); (b) a structural sense — simple agents aggregate into larger, more complex meta-agents (e.g., cells → organs).
2. **Nonlinearity** — the collective behavior of the system is not the simple sum of individual agent behaviors; interactions produce effects disproportionate to inputs, making the system's behavior harder to predict from its parts alone.
3. **Flows** — resources (information, energy, matter) move through the system along networks describable as {node, connector, resource}. Key associated ideas: the *multiplier effect* (how injecting a resource at one node ripples through and amplifies across the network) and the *recycling effect* (how reuse of resources affects overall system behavior).
4. **Diversity** — each agent/type occupies a niche defined by its interactions with other agents; removal of an agent type creates a "hole" that tends to be filled by new variants, so diversity is dynamically maintained rather than static.

**Mechanisms:**
5. **Tagging** — mechanisms (labels, markers, signals) that allow agents to identify, select, and discriminate among other agents, enabling selective interaction and the formation of aggregates/meta-agents. Tags "define the network" by delimiting which interactions occur.
6. **Internal models** — agents (or the system) build internal, often implicit, models of their environment/other agents that let them anticipate and evaluate the likely effects of actions. Holland distinguishes *tacit* internal models (implicit, guide current action without explicit forecasting) from *overt* internal models (used explicitly to explore alternative future scenarios).
7. **Building blocks** — reusable, recombinable sub-components/rules that can be assembled in different combinations to handle novel situations; decomposing a complex system into reusable building blocks allows it to generalize to new circumstances without having to encode every possible situation separately.

**Citations:**
- Holland, J. H. (1995). *Hidden Order: How Adaptation Builds Complexity*. Reading, MA: Addison-Wesley (Helix Books). ISBN 978-0201442304.
- Secondary sources consulted: Ferraioli, J., review of *Hidden Order*, Serendip Studio (serendipstudio.org/complexity/course/emergence06/bookreviews/jferraioli.html); additional WebSearch-indexed summary of the "seven basics" structure.

### 4. Yaneer Bar-Yam — *Dynamics of Complex Systems* (1997) and Siegenfeld & Bar-Yam, "An Introduction to Complex Systems Science and Its Applications" (*Complexity*, 2020)

**Source confidence:** Moderate. Direct PDF fetches failed for both the EOLSS "General Features of Complex Systems" article and the Siegenfeld & Bar-Yam 2020 arXiv paper (both returned corrupted binary content). Findings below rely on WebSearch-indexed abstracts/summaries and search-engine-surfaced excerpts, which were internally consistent but not independently cross-verified against full primary text.

**4a. Bar-Yam, *Dynamics of Complex Systems* (Addison-Wesley/Studies in Nonlinearity, 1997).** Described (per search-indexed summary) as the first textbook attempting a unified, discipline-spanning treatment of complex systems, using statistical mechanics, stochastic dynamics, cellular automata, and Monte Carlo methods as common tools across applications (neural networks, protein folding, ecosystems/organisms, human civilization). One directly retrieved characterization of Bar-Yam's core conceptual claim: **complex systems are composites of non-isolable parts** — i.e., what makes a system complex (rather than merely complicated) is precisely that it *cannot* be understood by decomposing it into independently-analyzable parts; the whole resists the standard reductionist method of decomposition. This is a distinctive and important framing: complexity is partly *defined* by resistance to decomposition/separability.

Central formal construct: the **complexity profile** — a function C(scale) that plots the amount of information needed to describe a system's behavior as a function of the spatial/temporal scale at which it is observed. This reframes "how complex is X?" as scale-relative rather than a single scalar number. [Note: full technical definition not independently verified from primary text in this pass; described consistently across two secondary sources as "the amount of information required to describe a system" as a function of observational scale.]

**4b. Bar-Yam, "General Features of Complex Systems"** (in *Encyclopedia of Life Support Systems* (EOLSS), UNESCO, 2002). Per search-indexed table of contents/structure, the article organizes around: Parts, Wholes, Relationships, Emergence, and Interdependence (under "Overview"); Self-Organizing Patterns; Complexity and Scale; and Evolution from Simple to Complex Patterns. This maps to a feature set of: **parts/wholes relationships, emergence, interdependence of components, self-organization, scale-dependence of complexity (complexity profile), and evolutionary increase in complexity over time.** [Section headings corroborated by WebSearch-indexed content; full body text not independently retrieved — flag as moderately, not fully, verified.]

**4c. Siegenfeld & Bar-Yam (2020), "An Introduction to Complex Systems Science and Its Applications," *Complexity*, vol. 2020, article 6105872. DOI: 10.1155/2020/6105872. Also arXiv:1912.05088.** Per the abstract (retrieved) and consistent secondary summaries, the paper's core principles are:
1. **Complexity profile** — same scale-relative information measure as above, now formalized further (a later companion paper, "Computationally Tractable Pairwise Complexity Profile," arXiv:1208.0823, extends this to high-dimensional real systems via pairwise dependencies).
2. **Efficiency–adaptability tradeoff** — systems face an inherent tradeoff: an *adaptable* system supports many independent, parallel actions/responses (higher complexity concentrated at smaller scales), while an *efficient* system has many parts acting in concert (lower complexity but coordinated action extending to larger scales). This is presented as a general principle governing how much "complexity" a system can/should have at a given scale.
3. **Matching complexity to environment** — a system must match the complexity of its environment (a restatement/generalization of Ashby's "law of requisite variety") to survive/function effectively; too-simple a system cannot cope with a complex environment, but excess internal complexity relative to the environment is wasteful.
4. **Multiscale analysis** — complex systems must be analyzed across multiple scales simultaneously, since relevant structure/behavior exists at more than one scale (contrasted with reductionist single-scale analysis).
5. **Evolutionary processes** — as a general mechanism by which complexity increases/changes over time, applicable beyond biological evolution to cultural, technological, and organizational change.

The paper explicitly frames itself as being about "general properties of systems as opposed to the modeling of specific dynamics" and is written for a broad, non-specialist audience (explicitly assuming only high-school-level math/science background), aiming at applications across "physical, biological, and social domains."

**Citations:**
- Bar-Yam, Y. (1997). *Dynamics of Complex Systems*. Reading, MA: Addison-Wesley (Studies in Nonlinearity). ISBN 978-0201557480.
- Bar-Yam, Y. (2002). "General Features of Complex Systems." In *Encyclopedia of Life Support Systems (EOLSS)*, UNESCO/EOLSS Publishers. [Chapter PDF: eolss.net/sample-chapters/c15/E1-29-01-00.pdf — retrieved but not machine-readable in this session.]
- Siegenfeld, A. F., & Bar-Yam, Y. (2020). "An Introduction to Complex Systems Science and Its Applications." *Complexity*, 2020, Article ID 6105872. https://doi.org/10.1155/2020/6105872. Also arXiv:1912.05088.

### 5. Paul Cilliers, *Complexity and Postmodernism: Understanding Complex Systems* (Routledge, 1998)

**Source confidence:** High. Corroborated across two independent, detailed secondary sources (a philsci-archive review article and a widely-shared practitioner blog summary "7 Characteristics of Complex Systems" by Sonja Blignaut, syndicated at both morebeyond.co.za and Medium), which converge almost verbatim on the list — strong evidence this accurately reflects Cilliers' text (commonly cited as appearing on pp. 3–4 of the 1998 book, per the philsci-archive review's page citation "CP: 3-4"). Direct PDF fetch of the uberty.org scan failed (binary/unreadable), so exact page numbers below rely on the secondary citation, not independent primary confirmation.

**The characteristics of complex systems (consolidated; commonly summarized as "seven," though the fuller academic review lists closer to nine distinct points):**

1. **Large number of elements** — complex systems consist of a large number of elements; taken individually, these elements can be simple.
2. **Rich interaction** — elements interact dynamically, exchanging energy or information; the interactions are numerous/rich (not sparse).
3. **Non-linear interaction** — even if a given element interacts only with a few neighbors, effects propagate through the whole system; interactions are non-linear, so small local interactions can have disproportionate system-wide effects.
4. **Feedback loops** — there are many direct and indirect feedback loops in the system.
5. **Open, far-from-equilibrium systems** — complex systems are open: they exchange energy/matter/information with their environment, and they operate under conditions far from thermodynamic equilibrium.
6. **Memory / history** — complex systems have memory that is not localized at any single point but distributed throughout the system's structure; consequently every complex system has a history, and that history is of "cardinal importance" to present behavior.
7. **Behavior determined by interactions, not components** — system behavior is determined by the *nature of the interactions*, not by what is contained within individual components; because interactions are rich, dynamic, fed-back, and nonlinear, whole-system behavior cannot be predicted by inspecting the components in isolation.
8. **Emergence** — used as the term for the (7) phenomenon above: organized, often surprising system-level behavior arising from component interactions that is not simply reducible to/predictable from the components.
9. **Adaptivity / self-organizing re-structuring** — complex systems are adaptive: they can reorganize their internal structure without the intervention of an external controlling agent.

(Some derivative summaries collapse this into a cleaner "seven characteristics" list by merging #7/#8 or #4/#6; the underlying substantive content is consistent across both derivative accounts found.)

**Citations:**
- Cilliers, P. (1998). *Complexity and Postmodernism: Understanding Complex Systems*. London: Routledge. ISBN 978-0415152877.
- Secondary sources consulted: "Review Article of Cilliers, P. 'Complexity and Postmodernism' (1998: Routledge)," philsci-archive.pitt.edu/1016/1/cilliers.htm; Blignaut, S., "7 Characteristics of Complex Systems" (morebeyond.co.za/7-characteristics-of-complex-systems/; cross-posted sonjablignaut.medium.com/7-characteristics-of-complex-systems-707e9a1d792a; also syndicated at stream.syscoi.com).

## Part (c): Merged Candidate Rubric Attributes

Deduplication logic: synonym clusters were merged into one candidate attribute (e.g., "spontaneous order" [LLW] = "self-organization" [Mitchell, Cilliers, Bar-Yam] = "order from disorder" [Mitchell paraphrase]). Each merged attribute below lists constituent synonyms, framework support count, and flags genuine conflicts.

| # | Merged attribute | Synonyms merged | Frameworks (of 5) | Genuine conflict / caveat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Numerosity / scale of components** | "many interacting components," "large networks" | 4 | None major — near-universal, but no framework gives a quantitative threshold ("how many is enough") |
| 2 | **Nonlinearity of interactions** | — | 5 | LLW explicitly call it *not necessary* even though widely cited — i.e., high prevalence in the literature does not equal consensus that it's *required* for complexity |
| 3 | **Feedback (loops, iterated causal coupling)** | — | 4 | None major |
| 4 | **Non-equilibrium / openness to environment** | thermodynamic openness, exchange with environment | 3 | Concept is most naturally physical/thermodynamic in origin (Prigogine-derived); its application to e.g. a *language* or a *stock market* is metaphorical, not literal, and needs a translated definition (see part d) |
| 5 | **Spontaneous order / self-organization** | "order from disorder," emergent macro-pattern formation | 5 | LLW call this a *necessary* condition; this is arguably the single most load-bearing, cross-framework-agreed feature |
| 6 | **Emergence** (macro properties/behavior not reducible to/predictable from components in isolation) | downward causation (LLW's stronger reading) | 5 | **Major conflict candidate**: "emergence" is used at least two different ways across frameworks — (i) weak/epistemic: emergent = hard to predict from parts without simulation; (ii) strong/ontological: emergent = genuinely novel causal powers not present in parts, with possible downward causation (LLW flag this second sense as philosophically contested). A rubric needs to pick one operational sense and hold it fixed across all ten catalog systems, or split into two columns. |
| 7 | **Robustness / resilience to perturbation** | redundancy-based persistence | 2–3 | LLW treat as *necessary*; other frameworks barely name it explicitly (though arguably assumed). Risk of conflating "robust" (resists change) with "adaptive" (embraces change) — these can pull in opposite directions for a given system and should NOT be merged into one column. |
| 8 | **Nested structure / hierarchy / modularity** | multi-scale organization, "complexity profile" scale-dependence | 3 | Bar-Yam's version is the most rigorous (formal complexity-profile-as-function-of-scale); LLW's and the others' are more qualitative/descriptive. Not a conflict, but a depth mismatch — Bar-Yam's version could in principle be made quantitative rather than rubric-scored, which is worth flagging to the parent project (this column might eventually migrate out of the qualitative/rubric slice into the quantitative slice for systems with good data, e.g., the Internet's autonomous-system hierarchy). |
| 9 | **History / memory / path dependence** | distributed memory (Cilliers) | 2 | Narrowly attested (only LLW and Cilliers name it explicitly), but conceptually it's arguably implicit in "adaptation" (Holland, Bar-Yam) and in any system with evolutionary dynamics — could be seen as redundant with #10, or as a distinct, importantly different property (memory without adaptation is possible, e.g. sedimentary rock strata "remember" history without "adapting" in any goal-directed sense). Recommend keeping distinct from adaptation. |
| 10 | **Adaptation / adaptive behavior** | learning, evolution, "efficiency-adaptability tradeoff" | 5 | **THE central conflict for this catalog.** LLW explicitly rule adaptation *neither necessary nor sufficient* for complexity — i.e., they treat it as common but not defining. Holland's entire framework (Complex *Adaptive* Systems) and Mitchell's working definition both build adaptation into the *definition itself*, effectively defining a subclass (CAS) that may not exhaust "complex systems" generally. Bar-Yam/Siegenfeld's efficiency-adaptability tradeoff treats adaptability as a dial a system can have more or less of, not a binary gate. **This directly matters for the catalog**: the Sun, the San Andreas Fault, and (mostly) Earth's climate are non-adaptive/non-agentic physical systems with no learning or evolutionary mechanism, whereas English, the NYSE, NYC, and the Internet are highly adaptive in Holland's/Mitchell's sense. Recommendation: score "adaptation" as a genuine rubric attribute (0 = no adaptive capacity, 4 = strong multi-level adaptive/evolutionary capacity) rather than a gating criterion for catalog inclusion — consistent with LLW's position that it's a typical-but-not-necessary feature, NOT with Holland's/Mitchell's CAS-specific framing. |

For each merged attribute, an assessment of whether a 0–4 rubric score is plausible for a **hurricane** (geophysical, non-agentic, short-lived), a **stock market** (socio-economic, agentic/reflexive), and a **language** (cultural-informational, agentic-but-diffuse, very long-lived) — as a proxy test for scorability across all six catalog categories (astrophysical, geophysical, physical-chemical, socio-economic, infrastructure/technological, cultural-informational).
