## 1. Chaos / sensitive dependence

**Verdict: HYBRID** (rubric score for "is chaos established / how strong is the evidence," with quantitative Lyapunov/predictability-horizon numbers cited as evidence where genuinely established — NOT as a clean cross-system comparable column, because the numbers are not commensurable across system types).

### What's solid

- **Atmosphere/weather.** The atmosphere is the canonical, well-established case. Estimates of the leading Lyapunov exponent for atmospheric models give a doubling time of ~2–5 days depending on method, translating into the famous **~2-week deterministic predictability limit** for weather, a result traceable to Lorenz's 1960s work and the "butterfly effect," and re-derived/confirmed in modern ensemble-forecasting studies. Charney et al. (1966) originally estimated the two-week limit from a 5-day doubling time; a widely cited modern estimate from atmospheric-model Lyapunov analysis gives λ₁ ≈ 0.03 per 3-hour step → doubling time ≈ 2.9 days → predictability horizon 1/λ₁ ≈ 2 weeks. This is genuinely quantitative, replicated across many studies, and operationally used (ECMWF/NOAA ensemble spread growth tracks it). Sources: Charney et al. 1966 (canonical); Krishnamurthy, "Predictability of Weather and Climate," *Earth and Space Science* 6 (2019), https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2019EA000586; "Exploring the Origin of the Two-Week Predictability Limit," *Atmosphere* 15(7):837 (2024), https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4433/15/7/837; "Lorenz's View on the Predictability Limit of the Atmosphere," *Meteorology* 3(3) (2023), https://www.mdpi.com/2673-8392/3/3/63.
- **Solar activity cycle.** Genuinely contested but quantitatively engaged, not folklore. Some nonlinear-time-series studies report low-dimensional chaotic signatures in sunspot records with very short Lyapunov-implied horizons (one estimate: ~15.9 months from sunspot-area analysis) [note: cite with caution — single-study result]; other studies find no evidence sunspots derive from low-dimensional deterministic chaos and treat the cycle as stochastically forced nonlinear dynamo behavior. Operationally, 1-cycle-ahead forecasts (the ~11-year cycle amplitude/timing) are attempted via precursor methods (e.g., polar-field precursors) with real but limited skill — this is NOT the same claim as "the Sun is low-dimensional chaotic." **Recommend citing predictive skill/horizon narratively, not a single Lyapunov number**, given the split literature. Sources: search results summarizing mixed sunspot chaos-detection literature; Petrovay, "Solar Cycle Prediction," *Living Reviews in Solar Physics* (review, ongoing series), https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.12648.
- **Financial markets.** This is the folklore-warning case. Early claims (Scheinkman & LeBaron, "Nonlinear Dynamics and Stock Returns," *Journal of Business* 62:311–337, 1989) reported nonlinear dependence and fractal-dimension estimates in weekly CRSP returns, popularly read as evidence of low-dimensional deterministic chaos. **This reading has been substantially walked back.** Subsequent BDS-test and denoising literature (e.g., "No evidence of chaos but some evidence of dependence in the US stock market," *Chaos, Solitons & Fractals*, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960077902003879, using >18,000 DJIA observations) finds nonlinear *dependence* (volatility clustering, GARCH-type structure) but **not** low-dimensional chaos; noise cannot reliably be separated from chaos with available tests, and the field consensus is "nonlinearity yes, low-dimensional chaos no." Isolated positive results persist in niche subseries (e.g., some FX tick data) but are not broadly replicated.

### Catalog recommendation
- Column should be **rubric 0–4** ("degree to which sensitive-dependence/chaos is established for this system, from stochastic/no evidence [0] to well-replicated deterministic chaos with measured Lyapunov exponent [4]") **plus a narrative/quantitative evidence field** for citing the specific horizon or exponent where one exists (atmosphere: ~2 weeks / λ₁≈0.03 per 3h; solar: contested, cite range; markets: explicitly flag as **debunked/contested**, do not report a Lyapunov number as fact).
- **Folklore warning:** Do not repeat "markets exhibit low-dimensional chaos" as an established fact — this is exactly the kind of complexity-science folklore the catalog must avoid. Cite Scheinkman & LeBaron as the historical claim, and cite the BDS/denoising literature as the correction.

## 2. Self-organized criticality (SOC)

**Verdict: RUBRIC-ONLY**, with quantitative *evidence* (power-law exponents) reportable as supporting narrative/citation but not as a clean, comparable column value, because "is this system SOC" is itself a contested classification, not a measurement.

Consulted: Watkins, Pruessner, Chapman, Crosby & Jensen, "25 Years of Self-organized Criticality: Concepts and Controversies," *Space Science Reviews* 198:3–44 (2016) [also arXiv:1504.04991], https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11214-015-0155-x. This review is explicitly critical/adjudicating, not promotional, and is the right anchor citation.

### Per-system status

- **Earthquakes — contested, not confirmed.** Bak & Tang (1989, *JGR* 94:15635) proposed earthquakes as SOC, but the Gutenberg-Richter power law itself predates SOC by four decades (empirical seismology, 1940s). Critics note SOC sandpile-type models (e.g., Olami-Feder-Christensen) fail to properly represent elastic stress transfer — displacement in the model is independent of event size, unlike real elastic solids — and some analyses find seismicity deviates from a true critical point (n_c ≠ 1) rather than sitting exactly at criticality. **Verdict: power-law size statistics (Gutenberg-Richter) are extremely well established; the SOC *mechanistic explanation* for them is contested and should not be presented as settled.** Sources: Bak & Tang 1989; critique summarized in SOC/earthquake literature reviews, e.g., https://faculty.sites.iastate.edu/tesfatsi/archive/econ308/tesfatsion/SandpileCA.Winslow97.htm and arXiv:cond-mat/0606118 (OFC model vs. real seismicity).
- **Solar flares — reasonably strong, actively researched.** Power-law flare-energy distributions are well documented and recently extended across an unprecedented 13 orders of magnitude (solar nanoflares E~10²⁴ erg to stellar superflares E~10³⁷ erg, using Kepler data), broadly consistent with fractal-diffusive SOC (FD-SOC) model predictions (α_F≈1.8, α_E≈1.67). However, a "dragon king" critique exists: the very largest events (e.g., extreme superflares) may belong to a distinct population/mechanism rather than the same power-law tail, which is itself a live debate about universality at the extreme tail. Sources: arXiv:2503.18136, "Self-Organized Criticality Across Thirteen Orders of Magnitude in the Solar-Stellar Connection"; Aschwanden et al., IOPscience 2019, https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/1538-4357/ab29f4.
- **Sandpiles / rice piles — the closest thing to a controlled positive result, but non-universal.** Frette et al.'s rice-pile experiments found that **elongated grains** produce power-law avalanche-size distributions (SOC-consistent), while **round grains** produce a characteristic scale (exponential/stretched-exponential, NOT SOC). This is important: it shows SOC is not automatic even in the literal sandpile-type system it was named after — it depends on microscopic grain geometry/dissipation mechanism. 1D exponent α≈1.53; 3D exponents τ≈1.21(2), cutoff dimension D≈1.99(2) reported in follow-up rice-pile studies. Sources: arXiv:cond-mat/9610010 (Christensen/Frette et al.); arXiv:cond-mat/0305592 (3D rice pile).
- **Financial markets — weak/contested.** SOC-style explanations for fat-tailed return distributions and volatility clustering have been proposed but are one of several competing explanations (alongside GARCH-type stochastic volatility, multifractal models, agent-based herding); no consensus that markets are SOC specifically as opposed to merely heavy-tailed/nonlinear. Treat as **contested, weakly evidenced** — this is a case the Watkins et al. review is explicitly skeptical about for many claimed SOC systems outside physics.
- **Rainfall** — power-law statistics reported in some precipitation/drought datasets, but causal SOC mechanism is even less settled than for earthquakes; treat as narrative-only.

### Catalog recommendation
- Rubric 0–4 for "strength of SOC evidence" (0 = no power-law evidence; 1 = power-law observed, no accepted critical mechanism; 2 = power-law + plausible SOC model, contested; 3 = power-law + well-supported SOC-consistent model, some anomalies; 4 = essentially consensus SOC — **note: no system in our catalog scores a clean 4**). Cite the Watkins et al. 2016 review as the arbiter of what counts as strong vs. weak evidence.
- **Folklore warning:** "Earthquakes are the textbook example of SOC" is folklore-adjacent — the power-law statistics are solid, the SOC *interpretation* is not settled science. Same caution applies to "sandpiles demonstrate SOC" — only for specific grain shapes.

## 3. Tipping points & early-warning signals

**Verdict: HYBRID.** "Number of identified tipping elements" is genuinely catalogable (a citable count from a specific synthesis paper) for climate; "critical slowing down observed yes/no" is catalogable as a categorical/rubric field per system where CSD has actually been tested empirically, but should not be assumed present just because a system has "tipping point" rhetoric applied to it informally.

- **Foundational papers, both verified:**
  - Scheffer, M., Bascompte, J., Brock, W.A., Brovkin, V., Carpenter, S.R., Dakos, V., Held, H., van Nes, E.H., Rietkerk, M., Sugihara, G. "Early-warning signals for critical transitions." *Nature* 461, 53–59 (2009). https://www.nature.com/articles/nature08227 (PubMed: 19727193). Proposes **critical slowing down** (rising variance + rising lag-1 autocorrelation as a system approaches a fold bifurcation), plus skewness change and "flickering" between states, as generic leading indicators across ecosystems, climate, and — explicitly — financial markets.
  - Lenton, T.M., Held, H., Kriegler, E., Hall, J.W., Lucht, W., Rahmstorf, S., Schellnhuber, H.J. "Tipping elements in the Earth's climate system." *PNAS* 105(6):1786–1793 (2008). https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.0705414105. Identified **9 candidate policy-relevant tipping elements** (Arctic sea ice, Greenland ice sheet, West Antarctic ice sheet, AMOC, ENSO, Indian summer monsoon, Sahara/Sahel–West African monsoon, Amazon rainforest, boreal forest).
  - Armstrong McKay, D.I., Staal, A., Abrams, J.F., Winkelmann, R., Sakschewski, B., Loriani, S., Fetzer, I., Cornell, S.E., Rockström, J., Lenton, T.M. "Exceeding 1.5°C global warming could trigger multiple climate tipping points." *Science* 377(6611):eabn7950 (2022). DOI: 10.1126/science.abn7950. PubMed: 36074831. Synthesized 200+ studies since 2008 and revised the list to **16 tipping elements** (9 global "core" + 7 regional "impact" — reporting varies slightly by source on the exact core/regional split, but 16 total is the figure widely reported by PIK, Carbon Brief, and the paper's own abstract framing of "a revised shortlist"). Found several tipping points already within their uncertainty range at ~1.1°C of observed warming.
- **This is genuinely quantitative/catalogable** for climate: "16 tipping elements identified (Armstrong McKay et al. 2022, updated from 9 in Lenton et al. 2008)" is a clean, citable number.
- **Other systems are much weaker.** The Scheffer et al. 2009 framework has been *proposed* for financial markets and other complex systems, but empirical CSD detection (rising autocorrelation/variance ahead of an actual measured regime shift) is far less established outside climate/ecology. For fault systems, some CSD-style precursor research exists (e.g., recent work on seismic catalogs testing critical-slowing-down indicators ahead of quiescence periods) but this sits closer to earthquake-prediction territory, which has its own strong debunking literature (Geller et al., "Earthquakes Cannot Be Predicted," *Science* 275:1616, 1997) — CSD-as-earthquake-precursor should be treated as exploratory/contested, not established.
- **No equivalent "N tipping elements" inventory exists** for the Sun, NYSE, Internet, ERCOT, Mississippi basin, NYC, or English — for these systems, "tipping points" language is metaphorical/narrative unless a specific bifurcation has been formally identified and modeled (e.g., grid voltage collapse has bifurcation theory behind it and could arguably be scored, but there is no canonical "N tipping elements of ERCOT" literature analogous to Lenton/McKay).

### Catalog recommendation
- Two separate sub-fields: (a) **quantitative count field**, fillable only for climate (cite Lenton 2008 + McKay 2022, state the count and the delta between the two syntheses); leave blank/N/A with justification for other systems rather than inventing an analogous list. (b) **rubric 0–4** for "empirical critical-slowing-down evidence observed for this system" (0 = untested/inapplicable framework; 4 = CSD robustly measured ahead of an actual documented transition). Climate subsystems (AMOC especially) and some ecological analogs score highest; most of our other systems will score low not because they lack tipping points but because CSD has not been rigorously tested there.

## 4. Cascading failure

**Verdict: HYBRID.** Power grids have real, if contested, quantitative blackout-size statistics; the Internet/BGP literature is much more theoretical/model-based with far fewer empirical outage-size distributions. No standard cross-domain "cascade-susceptibility metric" exists.

- **Power grids — the strongest quantitative case, but disputed.** Dobson, Carreras, Lynch & Newman, "Complex systems analysis of series of blackouts: Cascading failure, critical points, and self-organization," *Chaos* 17, 026103 (2007), DOI: 10.1063/1.2737822, https://pubs.aip.org/aip/cha/article/17/2/026103/934765/. Analyzing NERC and other national blackout datasets, they report the frequency of large blackouts follows an approximate power-law tail, with reported decay exponents roughly **1.3–2.0** for North American unserved-energy data (1984–1998) and similar ranges (~1.6–2.0) for New Zealand, China, and Norway; they interpret this as consistent with the grid being self-organized to operate near a critical loading point. **Important caveat:** M.E.J. Newman (the network-science Newman, not the blackout-paper's D.E. Newman) and others in the broader power-law-fitting literature (Clauset, Shalizi & Newman, "Power-law distributions in empirical data," https://arxiv.org/abs/0706.1062) have expressed general skepticism that many claimed empirical power laws — including blackout-size claims — are rigorously distinguished from alternative heavy-tailed distributions (log-normal, truncated power law) using proper statistical fitting (MLE + goodness-of-fit) rather than log-log regression by eye. **Recommend citing the exponent range with the explicit caveat that power-law-vs-alternative-heavy-tail is disputed.**
- **Internet/BGP — much weaker empirically.** There is real work on BGP cascading-failure *vulnerability* (routing table oscillation, peering-session drops under load, interdomain propagation delays), but this is dominated by simulation/graph-theoretic modeling (e.g., cascading failures in scale-free/heterogeneous network topologies) rather than a fitted empirical size-distribution comparable to the Dobson/Carreras blackout work. No equivalent "Internet outage size follows power law with exponent X, per Y years of data" claim was found with a clean primary citation. **This should be flagged as a gap: qualitatively well-studied, quantitatively much thinner than the power-grid literature.**
- **No standard cascade-susceptibility metric exists across domains.** Grid engineering uses N-1/N-2 contingency criteria and specific blackout-risk indices (unserved energy, etc.); network science uses percolation-threshold / giant-component-collapse metrics; these are not unified into one comparable number across our ten systems.

### Catalog recommendation
- **HYBRID column**: rubric 0–4 for "cascading failure documented and characterized" + quantitative sub-field citing the power-law exponent range where available (power grid: 1.3–2.0, Dobson et al. 2007, flagged as contested vs. alternative heavy-tailed fits) and narrative-only for systems (Internet, financial markets/NYSE circuit-breaker cascades, river-basin flood cascades) where only qualitative/simulation evidence exists.
- **Folklore warning:** Do not present "blackout sizes follow a power law" as an uncontested fact — cite both the finding and the Newman/Clauset-style statistical-rigor pushback.

## 5. Hysteresis / memory

**Verdict: HYBRID**, trending toward genuinely quantitative for the two best-documented cases (AMOC, traffic flow), narrative for the rest.

- **AMOC hysteresis — quantified in state-of-the-art GCMs, not just toy models.** van Westen, Jüling, Bathiany & Dijkstra (and related), "Asymmetry of AMOC Hysteresis in a State-of-the-Art Global Climate Model," *Geophysical Research Letters* (2023), arXiv:2308.14098, https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2023GL106088. A 4,400-model-year hysteresis simulation in a full GCM (not a conceptual box model) found a **hysteresis width of ~0.4 Sv** in freshwater forcing, with recovery occurring roughly 6× faster than collapse (asymmetric hysteresis loop) due to sea-ice feedbacks. This is a genuinely quantitative, citable, model-based number — flag clearly that it is **model-derived** (long transient GCM runs), not a measurement of the real ocean's hysteresis width, since the real AMOC has not been observed to complete such a cycle.
- **Ice-sheet hysteresis** — well established qualitatively (Greenland/WAIS bistability under different forcing directions is standard in the tipping-elements literature above) but specific hysteresis-width numbers are less standardized/more model-dependent than AMOC; treat as narrative with citation to the tipping-elements literature (Lenton 2008, McKay 2022) rather than inventing a single number.
- **Traffic flow hysteresis — genuinely empirically measured, real-world data (not just models).** The phenomenon (elliptical flow-density loops: congested→free-flow transition follows a different path than free-flow→congested) was first documented by Treiterer & Myers (1974) using aerial/video traffic data, and has since been given systematic measurement methodologies, e.g., "Hysteresis in traffic flow revisited: An improved measurement method," *Transportation Research Part B* 45(2):385–391 (2011), https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191261510001013. This is a strong, real-measured-data case — directly relevant to NYC as our city/infrastructure exemplar.
- **Financial markets** — "hysteresis" language is used loosely (e.g., persistent effects of shocks on volatility/liquidity regimes) but there isn't a clean, standard quantitative hysteresis-loop measurement analogous to AMOC or traffic; treat as narrative.

### Catalog recommendation
- HYBRID: rubric 0–4 for "hysteresis documented" + quantitative field where genuinely measured (AMOC: ~0.4 Sv hysteresis width, GCM-derived, van Westen et al. 2023; traffic: measurable loop area/asymmetry per Treiterer & Myers 1974 and Saberi & Mahmassani-type follow-ups). Explicitly distinguish **model-derived** (AMOC, ice sheets) from **directly measured from real-world observational data** (traffic) hysteresis, since conflating the two overstates confidence.

## 6. Entropy production / dissipation

**Verdict: RUBRIC-ONLY for climate (contested theoretical status), HYBRID for hurricanes (Carnot framing is quantitative and well accepted as an idealization), narrative-only/contested for cities and economies.

- **Climate — Maximum Entropy Production (MEP) hypothesis is real but unresolved, not a settled measurement.** Paltridge (1975, 1978) proposed that the climate system organizes toward a state of maximum entropy production, and simplified radiative-convective models tuned this way reproduce reasonable latitudinal temperature/cloud patterns. However, per Ozawa, Ohmura, Lorenz & Pujol's review, "The second law of thermodynamics and the global climate system: A review of the maximum entropy production principle," *Reviews of Geophysics* 41(4) (2003), https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2002RG000113, **MEP has no accepted first-principles derivation from the equations of motion**, is not derivable dynamically, and the climate-science community remains skeptical it is a "useful principle" beyond a curve-fitting device — later work (Ozawa & Ohmura) narrowed the claim to turbulent heat-transport entropy specifically, which fares somewhat better but is still not consensus. **This should absolutely not be presented as an established quantitative "entropy production rate of Earth's climate" column value** — it's a live theoretical controversy about whether MEP is even the right framework, not just an estimation-precision problem.
- **Hurricanes as Carnot heat engines — the most solid, genuinely quantitative case in this whole section.** Emanuel's (1986 and subsequent) framework treats the mature tropical cyclone as an approximately ideal Carnot heat engine: warm sea surface (~300 K) as heat source, tropopause (~200 K) as heat sink, giving a **theoretical maximum Carnot efficiency of roughly 1/3** ((300−200)/300), used directly to derive the maximum potential intensity (MPI) framework that is operationally used in hurricane-intensity forecasting. This is a well-accepted, quantitative, and *useful* (not just descriptive) application of thermodynamics to a real system — one of the few places in this entire slice where a genuine efficiency number is citable. In the mature storm, entropy production is dominated by frictional dissipation in the boundary layer rather than heat mixing. Sources: Emanuel, "Lecture 4: Physics of Mature Tropical Cyclones," MIT course notes, https://wind.mit.edu/~emanuel/geosys/node4.html; Physics Today review, "Hurricanes: Tempests in a Greenhouse," https://pubs.aip.org/physicstoday/article/59/8/74/398244/.
- **Cities/economies — thermodynamic treatments exist but are metaphorical/exploratory, not standard metrics.** "Urban metabolism" and energy-throughput accounting for cities (e.g., material/energy flow analysis) are real and quantifiable (tons of material, joules of energy per capita), but framing this as "entropy production of a city" in the physics sense is a much looser analogy without a standard, agreed measurement protocol; similarly for "entropy production of an economy." Treat as narrative only — do not force a number here without a specific, citable source per system, and be explicit that this is an analogy, not a measured physical quantity, for socio-economic systems.

### Catalog recommendation
- Split by system: **hurricanes get a HYBRID quantitative field** (Carnot efficiency ≈1/3 ceiling, Emanuel framework, well-accepted and operationally used); **climate gets RUBRIC + heavily caveated narrative** (MEP is contested theory, not measurement — score low on "established" axis regardless of how often it's cited, and cite Ozawa et al. 2003 review as the arbiter); **cities/NYSE/economies get narrative-only**, explicitly flagged as metaphorical thermodynamics unless a specific paper is found per system.
- **Folklore warning:** "The climate system maximizes entropy production" is a claim that sounds authoritative and gets repeated uncritically in pop-science treatments; the catalog must present it as a **contested hypothesis with real critics**, not settled physics.

## Three biggest uncertainties

1. **Whether a single rubric scale can honestly span both "well-measured physical quantity in dispute" (blackout power laws, MEP) and "no measurement framework exists at all" (city entropy, English-language cascading failure)** — these may need to be visually/structurally distinguished in the schema, not just scored differently on the same 0–4 scale.
2. **The solar-cycle Lyapunov/predictability-horizon figure (~15.9 months) came from a single study surfaced in search summaries, not a source I could directly open and verify in full** — flag as needing a follow-up direct-source check before it goes in the catalog; the qualitative "contested, split literature" characterization is solid, but I would not commit to that specific number without re-verification.
3. **No primary source was found for a quantitative Internet/BGP cascade-size distribution analogous to Dobson et al.'s blackout work** — I could not determine whether this is a genuine literature gap or whether I simply missed the right search terms (e.g., specific studies on the 2021 Facebook/major CDN outages or historical root-DNS cascade events might exist under different terminology and deserve a targeted follow-up search before concluding "Internet" should score lower than "power grid" on this attribute.
